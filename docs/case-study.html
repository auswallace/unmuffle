The pocket recording

A phone slid into a pocket mid-meeting, and one voice nearly vanished.

The recording: 7 minutes 37 seconds of iPhone video, two people talking. This is how the lost voice came back, and the tool it turned into, unmuffle.

The first problem was getting a model at all

Hugging Face, the usual source for Whisper, was blocked from both the cloud workspace and the laptop. The one model available offline, Vosk small, returned almost nothing after 2:08. The way through was the sherpa-onnx project, which publishes Whisper builds on GitHub, which was not blocked. From there: Whisper large-v3-turbo for the main pass and medium.en as a second opinion, both int8 on CPU.

Six attempts on the noisy half

  1. Spectral noise reduction

    Partial

    Learn the noise profile, subtract it, boost the level. The louder speaker came through in pieces. Whisper filled the rest with invented phrases.

  2. AI voice isolation (DPDFNet)

    Partial

    A DeepFilterNet model trained to keep voice and drop everything else. It helped the louder speaker and erased the quieter one as noise.

  3. Short overlapping windows

    Partial

    6 to 12 second slices, so Whisper stops skipping quiet stretches. Small gaps filled in. The 37 second gap came back as “so” and “Thank you.”

  4. Target the low band

    Failed

    The spectrogram showed nothing above 1.5 kHz in the gap, so the quiet voice was muffled. Isolating 120 to 1,100 Hz gave both models “[wind blowing]”.

  5. Subtract the two mics

    Breakthrough

    The two iPhone channels correlated at 0.88. The rumble was nearly identical on both and the voices were not. Left minus right cut the low-band rumble by about 12 dB, and words showed up in the gap.

  6. Adaptive canceller, per frequency

    Near word for word

    Learn, for each of 513 frequency bins and each 2 second block, exactly how the rumble differs between the mics, then subtract it. Fragments became full sentences.

Two mics hear the same rumble, not the same voice

The cancelled track came out mostly one speaker, and the normal mix mostly the other. That made speaker labels something you could measure.

From one recording to a tool

The manual process took an afternoon of trial and error. unmuffle runs the same steps on its own: drop in a file and a transcript folder appears next to it.

unmuffle run meeting.mov --names "Alex,Sam"
unmuffle watch ~/Desktop/to-transcribe

Every stage caches its work, so a stopped run resumes. Only one large model is in memory at a time, which keeps a 7.5 minute file under 4 GB.

The automated transcript is rougher on the louder speaker’s lines in the noisy half, which a person merged by ear. The flags land on exactly those lines.

What made the difference

  • Trust the listener over the model. Every model said the gap was empty. A person heard speech, and that was the reason to keep digging.
  • Look at the spectrogram. It showed where the voice lived, and why each method failed.
  • Keep every channel. Mixing to mono threw away the one difference that separated voice from rumble.
  • Make models disagree on purpose. Where two models agree, the words hold up. Where they don’t, a person should listen.

Built with ffmpeg, Whisper (large-v3-turbo and medium.en through sherpa-onnx), DPDFNet, pyannote segmentation, 3D-Speaker embeddings, and a frequency-domain canceller in NumPy. Everything runs locally.
